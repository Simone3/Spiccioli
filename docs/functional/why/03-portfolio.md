# §3 — Portfolio — why it is this way

*[Index](../README.md) · [the specification](../specs/03-portfolio.md)*

---

- **The decomposition separates money held from money put in, and both from the estimates laid on top of them.** An earlier version ran the first two together on one line called *cash and securities at cost*, and that was a compound name for a compound figure: a bank balance added to a cost basis, answering two questions with one number and admitting as much in its own title. Splitting them costs a line and no arithmetic — both halves were already inside it, and both are already summed for [§11.4](../specs/11-calculations.md#114-balances-and-net-worth) — and it leaves every line on the card a single kind of claim, which is what makes *read down and the estimation accumulates* a true description rather than an approximate one.
- **What is still not offered is a split by kind of asset.** That is the breakdown further down the same screen, and it is eleven rows by type rather than four by nature; the two answer different questions and neither is the other at lower resolution. The one figure that does appear in both is *pension funds, net*, which is a line here and a slice there because a pension fund is the only account that is a whole asset class on its own — and it is the same number in both places, computed once.
- **The pension fund is a line of its own because folding it in was telling a lie about the rest.** With pension funds on the cash side, a decade of fund revaluations — recorded, real, and on the largest single position in most files — sat inside a figure labelled *at cost*, while the line labelled *unrealised gain* covered only the brokerage and quietly claimed to cover everything. A line of its own puts every euro of estimation where it actually is: the first two lines are what the ledger recorded, the third is what the securities estimate, the fourth is what the pension estimates. It is also the honest place to put an asset that is illiquid in a way none of the others is.
- **The headline is a liquidation figure and now says so consistently.** Netting the securities and not the pension made it neither market value nor liquidation value — an asset was being counted at face precisely because it was the one that could not be sold. It is still an approximation over sources that behave very differently, and the four notes below it are what carry that; what it is not any more is an approximation with one arbitrary exemption in it.
- **One note became four because the one was describing two different things at once.** A caveat on the headline had to cover the securities and the pension in a single paragraph, and every sentence in it was true of one of the two and not of the other — a reader wanting to know what had been done to *their pension figure* had to sort a mixed list. Four notes, each on the line it is about, say the same things in the place each is true. The gain is not brevity, which is unchanged; it is that **two of the four now say that nothing has been done at all**, and a note on *Cash* stating that there is no rate and no haircut anywhere in it is worth more than its absence, which reads identically to an oversight. It also puts *read down and the estimation accumulates* on the screen: hover the four in order and the notes go from *nothing is estimated here* to two estimates in a row.
- **The headline is left with no note of its own** because it would have had to be either a fifth thing to read or a pointer to the four below it, and a caveat that says *see below* is a caveat nobody reads twice. The figure is the sum of four lines; it holds no estimation that is not on two of them.
- **The all-time card was renamed and totalled because it was a list of four figures with no stated relationship.** *All time* said when they were measured and nothing about what they had in common, so the card read as a miscellany — three category sums and a derived one — and the reader was left to notice that all four are money the banks, the funds and the market added or took while the portfolio sat with them. Naming it *gains and costs* says what the four are, and summing them answers the question the four provoke and never used to answer: **how much better off is this than a decade of cash under a mattress?** The total is the card; the lines are its working.
- **Value adjustments were the missing line, and their absence was the largest omission on the screen.** A pension fund's decade of growth is recorded, real, and in most files bigger than the fees, the taxes, the dividends and the realised gains put together — and it appeared nowhere as a lifetime figure, being folded inside *pension funds, net* on the card above and inside the Investments group on the report ([§6.1](../specs/06-categories.md#61-report)). A card about what keeping money somewhere earned that omitted the largest thing it earned was answering its own question wrongly.
- **The total is a sum of recorded facts and would stop being one if either estimate joined it.** The unrealised gain and the pension exit tax are the two obvious candidates and both were declined: the first is what the securities *would* make, the second is what the fund *would* pay, and a single figure mixing what happened with what would happen has no reading — it is neither a record nor a forecast, and it would move every time a price was entered. The estimates already have a card, one card up, and the split between the two is now the difference between them.
- **The realised-gain total states its omissions** because a total that silently omitted the sales with no cost basis would be the one figure on that card that is not what it says it is. **The card total repeats the count rather than inheriting it quietly**, since a figure built on an incomplete line is incomplete by exactly as much.
- **Closed accounts stay in every figure** because a closed account should be an empty one — check 11 fails by name while it is not ([§9](../specs/09-checks.md)) — so in a file whose checks pass it contributes nothing to net worth anyway, and in a file whose checks do not it contributes the balance that is really still there. The bank charged those fees, that interest was received, that sale really was made, and money left in a shut account is money the file still says you have.
- **Three of the card's four roles are read by this card and by nothing else** — `bank fees`, `wealth tax` and `interest and dividends`, and no check consults any of them ([§9](../specs/09-checks.md)). One of the three is *named* once more: [§11.6](../specs/11-calculations.md#116-derived-matching) says a transfer fee belongs in a category carrying `bank fees`, which is a rule about how to record rather than something the application reads. The category list is fixed ([§6.3](../specs/06-categories.md#63-category-list)), so the card could have named its categories directly and been correct forever — but every other part of the application that cares about a particular kind of money keys off `role`, and one screen quietly matching on names would have been the exception that made the rule untrue. Roles cost nothing and keep the promise whole.
- **The fourth, `value adjustment`, is the one the card shares**, and it is read three ways: check 10 prompts a revaluation on a pension fund that has not had one ([§9](../specs/09-checks.md)), [§11.3](../specs/11-calculations.md#113-hypothetical-liquidation) splits a fund's balance on it to find the exit-tax base, and this card sums it over the life of the file. **The three readings agree because they are the same sum taken over different scopes**, which is what makes adding the line free: the figure was already being computed, per account, for the arithmetic one card up.
- **Nothing on the card is counted twice, and one recording habit could make that untrue.** The five lines are four disjoint roles and a figure derived from trades rather than transactions, so no euro reaches two of them — except where a term deposit's accrued interest is entered as a value adjustment and then, on payment, entered again as interest ([§6.3](../specs/06-categories.md#63-category-list)) without the adjustment being reversed. That is a mis-recording rather than a defect in the card, no check reports it, and it is named here because the card is the one screen on which it would show up as a figure that is too large.
- **A negative or zero type keeps its amount and loses its slice** because a negative arc cannot be drawn, and pretending otherwise would leave a circle whose parts sum to more than the circle; a slice of no size is not a slice. The amount is the whole reason anyone would want the row, and a row saying so is worth more than a row that vanished.
- **Shares are computed against the positive types** so the column keeps adding to 100% while a negative type sits in it.
- **The pie and the list share one order** so that finding a slice in the legend is following a line rather than searching, and the whole column reads downwards from the largest contribution to the deepest hole. The pie exists to make the shape of the portfolio readable at a glance. This is the one table sorted by its own amounts because there are eleven rows at most, they are a shape rather than a ledger, and the shape is the entire point of drawing them.
- **The chart plots the headline quantity** because a chart sitting under a number and ending somewhere else would spend every reading being explained.
- **The breakdown by account totals to the headline** only because nothing is left out of it. **It borrows [§4.1](../specs/04-accounts.md#41-accounts)'s order rather than inventing one**, since it is the same twelve rows the Accounts screen shows and a reader who has learnt where a bank sits on one screen should not have to learn it again on the other. There is no third order in the application for the same list.
- **It is called a breakdown because that is what it is**, and calling it *Accounts* made it look like the Accounts screen rendered small. The screen above it splits net worth four ways by nature and further up by type; this splits the same figure by the account the money is in, and the three headings now read as three cuts of one number rather than as two breakdowns and a list.
- **The notes column went because a note is a property of the account and not of the balance.** It was the one column on the screen that was neither a figure nor the label of one — *matures 03/2027*, *household bills*, *moved everything to Fineco* — and it belongs where the account is edited ([§4.1](../specs/04-accounts.md#41-accounts)), which is one click away and is the only place it can be changed. Dropping it leaves a table whose every column is about the same arithmetic, and it takes the widest and most variable column out of one that has to fit beside an eleven-row pie. **It also makes the pair of screens symmetrical**, which they nearly were already: Accounts holds every stored field and refuses to compute a balance, and this table computes the balance and holds no stored field beyond the three that name the row.
- **The Balance column is the one that needed explaining, and it now does it in place.** It is the only figure in the application that is computed three different ways depending on the row — a sum of transactions, a sum less a tax, a sum of holdings' net proceeds — and until now a reader who wondered why a brokerage account showed a figure no statement of theirs carried had to find [§11.3](../specs/11-calculations.md#113-hypothetical-liquidation) to learn why. The note is on the header rather than on each row because it is one rule with three branches, not twelve facts, and **it is the same move as the four notes on the net worth card**: the explanation sits on the thing it explains.
- **The gross column was added because the note explained the haircut and never showed it.** A reader looking at a brokerage account knew, from the note, that a tax and a fee had been taken off — and had no way at all to see how much, short of reading two columns on Investments and doing the arithmetic for the pension fund by hand. The estimate of [§11.3](../specs/11-calculations.md#113-hypothetical-liquidation) is the largest unverifiable thing on the screen, and **a figure the application is careful to label an estimate everywhere should be one the reader can size**. Two columns side by side size it per account, which is the resolution at which it can be argued with: this fund's exit tax, that broker's fee.
- **It is the same figure twice on the six types that have no haircut, so those show a dash instead.** Repeating a current account's balance in both columns would say that something had been considered and come to nothing, when the truth is that nothing applies: money already money has no *before*. The em dash is the mark the application already uses for a column a record's type cannot carry — the institution of a `Cash` account, one column over — so it is a convention being reused rather than a symbol being coined.
- **The gross total counts the dashed rows all the same, and that is deliberate rather than an oversight.** It makes the column's total not the sum of the column as printed, which is the one thing about the table that has to be explained — and the explanation is worth it, because **it is what makes the two totals subtract to something**. The difference between them is the whole of the hypothetical tax and fees in the portfolio, in one figure, on the screen the estimate is on. A gross total that summed only the rows showing a figure would differ from the net total by the tax, the fees *and* every euro of cash in the file, which is a number with no reading at all. The note on the column header is where the screen says so.
- **Neither total became a fifth line on the net worth card.** The headline is a liquidation figure and the four lines beneath it are the decomposition of it; a gross figure up there would be a second headline saying what the portfolio is worth if the tax is ignored, which is precisely the reading the card was built to stop offering. Down in the breakdown it is a column of working, next to the accounts it is working on, and it says what it is for by where it sits.
