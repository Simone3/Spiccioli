# §6 — Categories — why it is this way

*[Index](../README.md) · [the specification](../specs/06-categories.md)*

---

- **The years filter is two values rather than a range** because the two questions a decade-long report is opened with are *how are things going* and *show me everything*, and a *from* and *to* pair asks the reader to express one of those in four clicks. *Last 5* is the default because a five-year window is wide enough to see a trend and narrow enough to stay readable at a glance on a screen whose rows are already twenty-seven. *All* is one click away and needs no arguments. The period filter on Transactions stays a real range ([§5.3](../specs/05-transactions.md#53-filters)), because that screen is where a particular fortnight is looked for; this one is read down a column.
- **An empty year in the middle keeps its column** because the sequence is what carries the meaning: a gap in the history should read as a gap, and a table that quietly closed up over the year somebody was between jobs would make the two adjacent years look consecutive when they are not.
- **The report's row order never moves.** Sorting by total would have put the biggest number at the top of each group, which reads well once and badly forever: change the year filter and every row jumps, so the eye has to find *Groceries* again instead of going straight to where it was last time. A table read every month is worth more when its shape is memorised, and that is only possible if the shape is a constant.
- **The category list and every picker are alphabetical instead**, because those are looked up in rather than read down: this tab is opened to see what type *Voucher top-up* is, whether *Rent & condominium fees* is receipt-tracked, why a figure landed where it did — the same action a picker serves, and the place to look a name up is the alphabet. The two orders serve two different actions and neither is improved by matching the other.
- **Investments is one group rather than three** because buying, selling and revaluing are one activity to a reader and three types only to the model — and because three groups of one category each, stacked, would have been three headings for three rows. It sits below Net because none of it is income or expenditure: a purchase is not a cost, it is the same money in a different shape. It answers the question Net provokes — *where did the surplus go?*
- **Amounts keep their signs** because absolute values with the direction implied by the group header would have read fine for Income and Expense and lied about the two groups that have no fixed direction: inside *Investments* a purchase goes out and a sale comes back, and a pension fund that lost value produces a negative value adjustment; inside *Internal* the two legs are a plus and a minus by definition.
- **Internal belongs above the line, not beside it.** Folding it into Net changes nothing over the whole row on *All*, which is precisely why it is safe, and a year filtered to one savings account should say the balance went up by what arrived — most of which is a transfer. The year-straddling transfer is the same property seen in one column: a December leg is money that left that year, and a Net that pretended otherwise would be describing a calendar the money did not keep to.
- **Net is defined in the specification because it is the one figure here whose name does the explaining.** *Income*, *Expense* and *Internal* are subtotals of rows the reader can see; *Net* is emphasised, is what the table exists to produce, and is a word people already have a meaning for — usually cash flow, occasionally taxable income, and it is neither. Leaving it to be inferred meant it would be inferred wrongly, and the wrong inferences are the ones that make somebody go looking for a reconciliation that does not exist. **Naming what it excludes costs three lines and settles it.** The asymmetry between dividends and capital gains is stated for the same reason: it is defensible and it is not obvious, so it is better read than discovered.
- **Investments carries no subtotal, and that is the point of it.** Securities purchase and sale are cash that moved through a bank account; a value adjustment is not — it is the pension fund being marked to its real value, and no money changed hands. Adding the three together would produce a figure that is neither the cash invested nor the change in what the investments are worth, and it would be smaller than the cash invested by exactly the revaluation. **A subtotal exists where something is built out of it**: Income, Expense and Internal have one because *Net* is their sum.
- **The cells are links** because the matrix always provokes the question *what is in there?* Without it the user has to reconstruct the filter by hand on another screen, which is the sort of small friction that ends with a spreadsheet being opened instead. The subtotals and Net are not links because no filter expresses them: the category filter takes one category ([§5.3](../specs/05-transactions.md#53-filters)), and *Net* is a sum of sums rather than a selection. **Nothing is offered that cannot then be shown**, and that is also what decides what a row total hands over: it is the sum of the columns on screen, so the period it sets is the span of those columns. Clearing the period instead would be right only while the table happened to be showing every year — which is what *All* does and *Last 5* does not, the two differing by however much history sits before the window. A link that selected a different set from the figure that was clicked would be worse than no link, because the discrepancy is invisible at both ends.
- **Closed accounts stay in the report** because the money that went through an account shut in 2021 went somewhere: leaving it out would make every year before that one disagree with itself depending on what has been closed since.
- **Rules sit on this screen** because what a rule change does is move figures in this report, and the two are worth being a tab apart.
- **The unit of work is the edit session, not the keystroke.** A rule list is reasoned about as a whole — a new rule usually wants to sit above an existing one, which means an edit and a drag that are only correct together — and applying each half as it happens would re-categorise the file twice, the first time into a state nobody asked for. It is also the only way a drag can be part of the same decision as the rule it moves. A draft is the one thing in the application that is not in the file the moment it is typed, so it is the one thing that has to be asked about before it can be lost.
- **The *Applies to* count describes the file rather than the draft** because a count that silently switched between describing the file and describing a proposal would be the one number on the screen nobody could trust.
- **The summary is four totals and no breakdown by edit.** A line per edit looks like the more informative version and is the less honest one: first match wins, so a row a move and a deletion both touched can be attributed to either, and the attribution would be a choice the summary made rather than something the file says. The question being answered is not *what did each edit do* but *what will the file look like if I press this*, and four figures answer it exactly. The edits themselves are already on the list behind the panel, marked as added, moved and deleted.
- **There is no per-rule tester** because the question worth answering is what the *list* will do, and a panel that answered it one rule at a time could not answer it for a drag at all — reordering is the edit most likely to change a category, and it has no rule under edit to hang a preview on. One summary over the whole draft answers what no per-rule count could.
- **The 95% target is what makes the absence of a bulk edit tolerable** ([§5.6](../specs/05-transactions.md#56-selecting-and-deleting-in-bulk)): if a quarter of the file needed touching by hand, the rules would be the thing that was wrong.
- **What an apply costs.** One apply is one pass of several dozen rules over ten years of transactions, and the pass that produces the summary is the same pass that produces the result — compute it once, show the figures, and write what was already computed if the user confirms. Nothing is recomputed on confirmation. That is the whole reason the summary is affordable at all, and the reason the list is applied once at the end rather than re-run behind every keystroke.
- **The category list tab is worth a tab even though nothing on it can be changed**, because a category's type decides which group of the report it lands in and whether it counts towards Net, and being able to look that up without reading this document is worth something. The live transaction count is what makes it more than decoration.
- **`Role` and `order` are not on it**, though both are stored and both are read constantly. They are how the application is wired rather than anything true about the money: a role is the handle a check grabs a category by, and `order` exists so one table can sort on one field. Neither can be set from any screen, so a column of them is a row of answers to a question the reader did not ask — and eighteen blank roles read as a setting somebody forgot to fill in, which is the one reading that is actively wrong. The tab keeps what a reader can act on: the type, whether a receipt is expected, and how many transactions are in there.
- **Check 13 failing straight after an import is the point.** The rent that was just imported *is* outstanding until someone has looked for the invoice, and the check is the list of what to look for. A row typed by hand can be given its state on the form and need never appear in that list at all, which is the difference between one row and two hundred. An application that set the state to `pending` on the user's behalf would have been guessing, and one that set it to `checked` would have been lying; the only honest default is the one that says nobody has been near this yet. The migration script setting each historical row's state is what makes check 13 on day one report what is genuinely outstanding rather than the whole decade.
- **A sign contradicting its category type is not flagged** because a refund routinely zeroes out a purchase inside an expense category.
